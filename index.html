Jiaqi Cai · Contemporary Korean Cinema

Unequal Lives in a Vertical City

How do contemporary South Korean films and documentaries use urban space to represent class inequality and social exclusion?

Background image: Seoul cityscape, Unsplash. Used for educational presentation.

The question

Who has room to live securely?

South Korea’s economic growth is visible in Seoul’s modern infrastructure. Yet prosperity has not given all residents secure housing or stable work. In these films, wealth is shown through space: sunlight, privacy, height and freedom of movement. Poverty appears through semi-basements, temporary rooms, distant neighbourhoods and places marked for redevelopment.

HIGHPrivate gardens, stylish apartments and control over space
MIDDLETemporary access to work, comfort and social respectability
LOWSemi-basements, insecure rooms, eviction and public shelters
This website argues that urban space makes class inequality visible. Parasite turns height and housing into a class map; The Remnants shows the real human cost of redevelopment; Microhabitat and Burning reveal less dramatic but persistent forms of insecurity.
Framework

Compressed modernity

Chang Kyung-Sup’s idea of “compressed modernity” helps explain the pressures behind these images. South Korea’s economic and social transformation happened within a relatively short period. Rapid development created impressive infrastructure, but it also intensified competition, housing pressure and uneven access to security. The films do not reject modernisation itself. Instead, they ask who carries its costs.

Urban space is therefore more than a background. Homes, roads, stairs and redevelopment zones organise relationships between people. They decide who can remain private, who is exposed, who can move easily and whose presence is treated as a problem. By placing characters in sharply different environments, cinema makes structural inequality part of everyday experience.

Case study 01

Parasite

A city divided by height

Film still from Parasite (Bong Joon-ho, 2019), reproduced by Aju Business Daily for discussion of semi-basement housing.

Two homes, two versions of Seoul

The clearest class contrast is between the two families’ homes. The Parks live in a spacious house on higher ground. The Kims occupy a semi-basement flat with a window almost level with the street. The difference is not only size. The Park home offers sunlight, privacy and a garden separated from the city. The Kim family sees rubbish and drunk passers-by outside. They belong to Seoul, but occupy a part of it that others prefer not to notice.

Movement

Going up does not mean moving up

The Kims walk uphill to work in the Park house and descend a long sequence of stairs when they return home. They can enter a wealthy space and briefly perform middle-class roles, but they never gain control over that space. Their journey downwards turns class into physical movement. The city itself seems to return them to their social position.

Access to a rich home is temporary; the journey home restores the class boundary.

The rainstorm

One storm, unequal consequences

The storm exposes how differently residents experience the same city. For the Parks, the rain is an inconvenience followed by clear weather and an outdoor party. For the Kims, it floods their home, destroys their possessions and forces them into a public shelter. What appears natural is shaped by housing and infrastructure. The film shows that vulnerability is not shared equally: comfort for one family exists beside disaster for another.

Choi’s work on South Korean cinema helps explain why this criticism appears through suspense, comedy and striking design rather than a purely realistic style. These popular elements make class inequality easy to see, although they also risk turning poverty into spectacle.
